Leave Balance had been sold on flat per-company tiers since day one — under fifty employees, under two hundred, unlimited. In June the decision came down to move to per-employee pricing at renewal, and it was not a decision the product team was being consulted on. The unit economics were genuinely broken at the top tier, where a handful of two-thousand-person customers were paying the same as a two-hundred-person one and generating forty times the support load. I agreed with the change. That didn't make the Monday any easier.

The people who would live with it were the four-person support team, the two customer success managers, and the engineers who'd have to build proration, seat-count reconciliation, and a mid-cycle upgrade flow in six weeks. The default plan was a thirty-minute all-hands where leadership explains the rationale and asks if there are questions, and where the only questions asked are logistical because nobody asks the real question in a thirty-minute all-hands.

We ran a **Conversation Cafe** instead, the week before the customer emails went out.

## The structure

Conversation Cafe is built for exactly this: a group absorbing something disruptive that they didn't choose and can't reverse. Tables of five to seven, a talking object per table, and four rounds.

**Round one**, the object passes around the circle and each person speaks once, uninterrupted, on the invitation question. **Round two**, it goes around again — this time responding to what they've heard. **Round three** is open conversation with the object in the middle, available to anyone who wants uninterrupted space. **Round four** is a closing circuit: what each person is taking away.

The agreements get read out before round one and they matter more than usual when the topic is a decision people dislike: suspend judgment, listen to understand, speak from your own experience rather than on behalf of an absent group, go for depth, and use the object.

Ninety minutes for eleven people at two tables. No slides. I facilitated the agreements and then said essentially nothing for an hour and a half, which is harder than it sounds and is the job.

## Running it on the pricing change

The invitation question was: **"When this lands with customers, what will be hardest — and what do you know that the rest of us don't?"**

The second clause was deliberate. Support people sit on an enormous amount of specific knowledge that never reaches a pricing decision because nobody has ever built a route for it. Asking for it explicitly, in a format where they get uninterrupted airtime, is a route.

Round one at my table was mostly braced-for-impact — three people naming the accounts they expected to lose, which was useful and not surprising. Round two produced the finding of the session. One of the CSMs, holding the object, worked out loud through something she'd half-known for months: that a large share of our biggest accounts have seat counts in the HR system that are wildly wrong, because nobody ever had a reason to clean them up. Offboarded employees stay flagged active for months. Seasonal staff at two of our retail customers triple the headcount every November.

Under flat tiers, that was a data hygiene curiosity. Under per-employee pricing, it's an invoice that says a customer owes us for four hundred people who left last year — and the first thing that customer will do is not pay it, and the second thing is question every number we've ever shown them.

Nobody in the pricing decision had known that. It came out because a talking object gave one person ninety uninterrupted seconds to finish a thought she'd never had reason to finish.

The open round surfaced the other half: support's real fear was less about churn than about being the ones to explain a change they'd had no part in, to customers who'd ask them if it was fair. One of the newer reps said she didn't know what she was allowed to say if a customer asked whether *she* thought the pricing was reasonable. That question got a real answer that afternoon and became a line in the internal FAQ.

We shipped a seat-count reconciliation tool and a ninety-day grace period on inflated counts before the pricing change went live. Both came directly out of that room. The decision itself did not change, and I said so at the top — but the rollout did, substantially, and that's a real thing to be able to influence.

## Why the format survives a non-negotiable topic

The obvious objection is that running a listening structure on a settled decision is manipulation with extra steps. It is, if you pretend the decision is open. It isn't, if you're explicit about what's fixed and what isn't.

What Conversation Cafe actually does here is separate two things that a normal all-hands fuses: *processing the news* and *reacting to the plan*. In a Q&A, the only available move is to challenge the decision, so people either challenge it uselessly or say nothing. In a talking-object circle, the frame is sense-making, so the material that comes out is knowledge rather than objection — and knowledge is the thing you can actually act on.

The rounds do the sequencing. Round one drains the immediate reaction, which people need to say before they can say anything else. Round two, after hearing everyone, is where the specific operational knowledge appears. Skip round two and you get a room full of vented feelings and no seat-count discovery.

## When to skip it

Skip it if you're not prepared to change anything. If the rollout, the messaging, the timeline, and the tooling are all locked, then this is a ninety-minute performance of consultation and everyone in the room will identify it as one by round three.

Skip it if the group is too large to split into real tables of five to seven, unless you have a facilitator per table who understands that their job is the agreements and nothing else.

And don't run it *instead of* explaining the decision. The rationale has to be delivered plainly first, by someone who owns it, before anyone can meaningfully make sense of it. Conversation Cafe metabolizes news; it isn't a way of avoiding delivering it.
