A roadmap shows what a team intends to do. It rarely explains why one option was chosen over another. A short decision log fills that gap, especially when the same question returns months later.

Use it for choices with a meaningful tradeoff: a scope cut, an integration boundary, or a decision to keep a workflow manual. Routine reversible details do not all need a formal entry.

## Lead with the decision

State the choice plainly and identify its owner. Include the date and the scope in which it applies. “Use a manual review for initial imports in the pilot” is more precise than “imports require review.”

The narrower statement leaves room for the policy to change outside the pilot without making the original decision wrong.

## Preserve the alternatives

Record the serious options considered and the reason they were not selected. Avoid caricaturing alternatives to make the chosen option look inevitable.

For a hypothetical import workflow, the options might be full automation, manual review, or restricting supported formats. Each carries a different cost in user effort, operator time, and implementation complexity.

## Separate evidence from assumptions

List what the team observed and what it expects. “Three pilot imports required field mapping corrections” is evidence if documented. “Most future imports will have the same issue” is an assumption.

Keep uncertainty visible. A decision can be reasonable under incomplete information without pretending the missing information is known.

## Name the accepted cost

Every option gives something up. If manual review delays onboarding, write that down. If a narrow format reduces implementation effort but excludes some users, preserve that consequence.

This makes later evaluation fairer. A known cost is not necessarily a surprise failure; it may be the tradeoff the team deliberately accepted.

## Add a revisit condition

Use an event or observation that would change the decision: operator effort exceeds the available capacity, a new customer segment requires another format, or the pilot ends. Avoid arbitrary review dates when an operational trigger would be clearer.

When the decision changes, link the new entry rather than rewriting the old one to look prescient. The log should explain what was reasonable with the information available at the time.

A useful decision log reduces repeated debate without freezing judgment. It preserves the reasoning well enough that the next discussion can start from new evidence instead of reconstructing old conversations.
