The plan for Q4 was to rewrite the Recap CRM mobile app. Not refactor — rewrite, from a three-year-old hybrid stack to native, with the deal pipeline and activity feed as the first surfaces. It was a defensible plan. I'd written the doc, the doc had numbers in it, and in the planning meeting fourteen people had agreed with it in the way people agree with a plan that has numbers in it.

I did not believe the agreement. Not because I suspected anyone of dishonesty, but because "does anyone have concerns" is the least effective question in product management, and a room that has just read a well-argued doc will not produce its real reservations in response to it.

So before the detailed planning session, we ran twelve minutes of **Mad Tea**.

## What it is

Mad Tea is the fastest structure in the Liberating Structures set and the one that looks least like work. Everyone stands in two concentric circles, inner facing outer, so each person has a partner. The facilitator reads a **sentence stem** aloud. Both partners complete it — fifteen to thirty seconds each, no discussion, no follow-up questions. Then the circles rotate by one or two positions, everyone has a new partner, and the next stem is read.

Nine or ten stems, twelve to fifteen minutes, and it's over before anyone has settled into a posture. The pace is the point: at thirty seconds you say the first true thing rather than the third careful thing.

The stems carry the design. They should be short, open, and provocative enough to require a genuine answer. "The thing I'm most curious about is…" "What worries me most is…" "If I could change one thing tomorrow it would be…" "Something we're not talking about is…" You write them for the specific situation; the generic set works but the specific set works far better.

There is no output artifact. Nothing is captured, nothing is voted on. That unsettles people the first time, and it's deliberate — capturing turns a fast honest exchange into an on-the-record statement, and the honesty is the product.

## Running it before the rewrite kickoff

Fourteen people, two circles, my stems written the night before:

1. What excites me about a native rewrite is…
2. What worries me most is…
3. Something nobody has said out loud yet is…
4. In six months I'd be disappointed if…
5. The part I don't understand is…
6. If we had to cut scope, the first thing I'd cut is…
7. The person or team I'm most worried about in this plan is…
8. What would make this obviously worth it is…
9. What I need in order to do my part well is…

Stem three broke it open. I was standing in the outer circle participating — which I recommend, since a facilitator hovering with a notepad kills it instantly — and I heard three different versions of the same thing pass by me in thirty seconds: that we had never established whether mobile usage was actually growing, and that the rewrite doc's central metric was session count, which had been flat for a year.

Stem six was the other one. "The first thing I'd cut" produced, from at least five people I could hear, the activity feed — which was scheduled as one of the two launch surfaces. Nobody had objected to it in the planning meeting. In thirty seconds with one partner, most of the room independently identified it as the least valuable half of the plan.

And stem seven, which I'd nearly cut for being too pointed, surfaced something I'd been oblivious to: several people named the two-person design team, who were being asked to produce a full native design language while continuing to support web. That was a real staffing problem hiding inside an architecture decision.

Twelve minutes. No notes, no summary, no action items from the structure itself. Then we went into the planning session, and I opened it by saying that I'd heard three things standing in that circle and wanted to put them on the table: the session-count metric, the activity feed, and design capacity.

That's the move. The structure surfaces the material; naming it yourself, immediately, gives everyone permission to discuss what they've all just said privately to two or three people. The plan we left with launched pipeline only, added a two-week instrumentation spike before any rewrite work, and had a designer contract attached to it.

## Why speed produces honesty

Thirty seconds is too short to construct a position. It's long enough to say the thing you already think and not long enough to package it, and the packaging is where candor is normally lost. Give someone three minutes to answer "what worries you" and you get a balanced, considerate answer that has already accounted for how it will land. Give them thirty seconds and you get the worry.

The rotation matters as much as the clock. Each stem is answered to a different partner, so nobody is building a case with one person across the session — which means nobody feels like they've committed to a position. It stays low-stakes, and low-stakes is where people say the inconvenient thing.

And the volume is what makes it legible. Fourteen people times nine stems is a hundred and twenty-six spoken answers in twelve minutes. You cannot record that, and you don't need to: what you notice, standing in the circle, is repetition. Hearing the activity feed named three times by three different people in one rotation is a signal no survey would have given me and no planning meeting would have produced.

## When to skip it

Skip it when you need a decision or a record. Mad Tea produces neither and it's not sulking about it — that's the design. Use it to open something, never to close one.

Skip it with a group in genuine distress. The pace that makes it candid also makes it glancing, and asking someone to summarize a painful situation in thirty seconds and then rotate is dismissive. [Conversation Cafe](/product-management/conversation-cafe-generated-assertions-incident-shortest/) is the structure for that shape.

Skip it if the stems are bland. "What I hope for this quarter is…" gets you nothing. The stems have to permit an uncomfortable answer, or you'll spend twelve minutes hearing the plan read back to you in a hundred and twenty-six small pieces.

And it needs a real room or a video tool that can reshuffle pairs fast. If your reshuffle takes ninety seconds, the structure's whole tempo collapses and you've built a slow structure with a fast one's shallow prompts — the worst of both.
