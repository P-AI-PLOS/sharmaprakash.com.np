Every IEM review sorts earphones into groups: neutral, bassy, bright, V-shaped, vocal. Those labels are useful, but they are one reviewer's buckets, not a standard. Underneath them is one idea, the **sound signature**: how loud the bass, mids and treble are relative to each other.

This post is the reference for the rest of the series. Once you know which signature you like, choosing an IEM, a source and the settings on your player gets much easier.

## Frequency response in one paragraph

Human hearing runs from about 20 Hz to 20 kHz. Reviewers split that range into rough bands:

| Band | Range | What lives there |
|---|---|---|
| Sub-bass | 20–60 Hz | Rumble, kick-drum weight, the feel of a bass line |
| Mid-bass | 60–250 Hz | Punch, warmth, body of bass guitar and male voices |
| Mids | 250 Hz–4 kHz | Voices, guitars, pianos: most of the music |
| Treble | 4–20 kHz | Cymbals, sibilance, air, sense of detail |

A frequency-response graph plots how loud an IEM plays each of those frequencies. A sound signature is the shape of that graph, described in words.

## The signatures

| Signature | Shape | Sounds like | Suits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Neutral / reference** | Close to flat against a target | Close to the chosen reference target | Purists, mixing, judging recordings |
| **Harman** | Neutral mids, a sub-bass shelf, smooth treble lift | Balanced with weight underneath | A reference to compare with your preference |
| **Warm** | Extra mid-bass and lower mids, relaxed treble | More lower-frequency emphasis | Long sessions, acoustic, older recordings |
| **Dark** | Warm, with treble pulled back further | Thick and soft; little sparkle | Treble-sensitive ears, bright recordings |
| **Bright** | Treble forward | Airy, detailed, sometimes fatiguing | Detail hunters, classical, live recordings |
| **Bass-heavy (L-shape)** | Big bass, the rest even | Thumping, powerful | Hip-hop, EDM, commuting |
| **V-shape** | Bass and treble up, mids down | Exciting and fun; vocals step back | Pop, rock, gaming |
| **U-shape** | A gentler V | Fun without losing vocals | All-rounders who like some excitement |
| **W-shape** | Bass, mids and treble all lifted | Lively and forward | Energetic genres, people who find neutral dull |
| **Mid-centric / vocal** | Mids forward | Voices up front and intimate | Singer-songwriters, ghazal, podcasts |

### Neutral is not the same as flat

A flat raw measurement is not the same thing as matching a neutral target. Your outer ear and ear canal boost some frequencies on their own, and IEMs sit past part of that. So "neutral" means *neutral against a target curve* that accounts for how ears work, not a flat line on a graph.

### Where Harman comes from

Harman International, the company behind JBL, AKG, Harman Kardon and many car audio systems, ran large blind listening tests through its research group led by Sean Olive. Listeners preferred a tuning that is mostly neutral, with a gentle bass shelf and smoothly lifted treble. Harman published that curve as a target for headphones and later for IEMs.

Many reviewers and makers now use the Harman target, or a close relative of it, as their reference point. An IEM described as "Harman-tuned" was tuned towards that research curve; it does not have to be made by Harman.

## Choosing a preference

A genre does not dictate a tuning. Use familiar recordings to describe what you want more or less of, then compare [graphs from the same measurement setup](/technical-notes/reading-iem-frequency-response-graphs/). Check [fit and seal](/technical-notes/iem-fit-eartips-and-cables/) before attributing weak bass to the earphone.

## Try before changing equipment

Use a small EQ adjustment to explore a preference, keeping volume comfortable and avoiding clipping. Change one band at a time. A description such as warm or bright is shorthand, not a promise of how a particular recording will sound to you.
